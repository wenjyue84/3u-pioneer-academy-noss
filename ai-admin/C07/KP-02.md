## Objektif Pembelajaran / Learning Objectives

Upon completion of this Information Sheet, trainees will be able to:

1. Explain the five core pillars of Responsible AI: fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, and human oversight.
2. Describe what AI hallucination is, how to detect it, and how to mitigate its impact in a workplace setting.
3. Identify three forms of AI bias that can affect HR and office administration decisions.
4. Explain the intellectual property (IP) considerations when using AI-generated content for business purposes.
5. Draft the key components of an AI Usage Policy (Polisi Penggunaan AI) for a Malaysian SME.
6. Describe the structure of an AI governance committee and the roles within it.

## 1. Responsible AI — The Five Pillars (Lima Tiang AI Bertanggungjawab)

Responsible AI is the practice of designing, deploying, and using artificial intelligence systems in ways that are ethical, safe, and aligned with human values. International bodies, governments, and technology companies have converged on a set of core principles, which are reflected in Malaysia's emerging AI governance policy.

### Pillar 1 — Fairness (Keadilan)

AI systems must not produce outcomes that **discriminate** against individuals or groups based on race, religion, gender, age, disability, or other protected characteristics. In a Malaysian workplace context:

- An AI resume screening tool trained predominantly on data from one ethnic or educational background may unfairly rank candidates from other backgrounds lower.
- An AI-generated performance evaluation that correlates negatively with languages other than English may disadvantage non-English-speaking employees.

**What organisations should do:** Regularly audit AI outputs for patterns that suggest differential treatment. Where bias is detected, the tool should be reconfigured, retrained, or replaced.

### Pillar 2 — Transparency (Ketelusan)

Individuals affected by AI decisions have a right to **know that AI was used** and, where the decision is significant, to receive an explanation.

| Situation | Transparency Requirement |
|---|---|
| AI-assisted resume screening | Disclose to applicants that AI tools were part of the selection process |
| AI-generated customer service responses | Identify chatbot responses as AI-generated, not human |
| AI-assisted performance reviews | Inform employees that AI analytics contributed to the assessment |
| AI-generated marketing content | Disclose when content published externally was substantially AI-generated (MCMC guidance) |

### Pillar 3 — Accountability (Akauntabiliti)

Every AI output used for a business decision must have **a named human who takes responsibility** for reviewing and approving it. "The AI decided" is not an acceptable answer when a wrong decision harms someone.

In practice, accountability means:
- Maintaining **audit logs** of AI-assisted decisions (who used the tool, what input was given, what output was used)
- Designating a **human reviewer** for all high-stakes AI outputs (HR decisions, financial approvals, legal documents)
- Establishing escalation procedures when AI output is disputed

### Pillar 4 — Privacy Protection (Perlindungan Privasi)

AI systems must handle personal data in compliance with PDPA 2010 (covered in KP-01). In addition, the principle of **privacy by design** requires that privacy protections be built into AI system selection and configuration, not added after the fact.

Privacy protection in AI deployment includes:
- Choosing AI tools that offer enterprise-grade data processing agreements
- Configuring AI tools to minimise data collection (data minimisation)
- Restricting access to AI tools that process personal data to authorised personnel only
- Never using real personal data in AI demonstrations, training sessions, or testing environments — use anonymised or synthetic data instead

### Pillar 5 — Human Oversight (Pengawasan Manusia)

AI must operate **under human supervision**, particularly for decisions that affect individuals significantly. Fully autonomous AI decisions without human review are inappropriate in most office administration contexts.

Categories requiring mandatory human oversight in the office:

| AI Application | Why Human Oversight is Essential |
|---|---|
| AI-drafted letters and contracts | Legal liability; AI may misstate facts or omit key terms |
| AI-assisted HR decisions | Employment law compliance; risk of bias |
| AI financial projections | Accuracy risk; business decisions depend on these figures |
| AI-generated regulatory filings | Legal obligations; errors may attract penalties |
| AI-generated communications to clients | Reputational risk; accuracy and tone must be verified |

## 2. AI Hallucination — Risk and Mitigation (Halusinasi AI)

### 2.1 What Is AI Hallucination?

**AI hallucination (halusinasi AI)** occurs when a generative AI model produces output that sounds plausible and confident but is **factually incorrect, fabricated, or unsupported** by the underlying data. Hallucinations arise because large language models predict the most statistically likely next word or phrase — they do not "look up" facts from a verified database.

Common types of hallucination:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Factual fabrication** | AI states that "the PDPA 2010 was amended in 2019" when the actual amendment year is different |
| **Citation invention** | AI cites a legal case or academic paper that does not exist |
| **Numerical error** | AI generates financial figures or percentages that have no basis in the source data |
| **Confident misstatement** | AI incorrectly states a company's registration number, a person's job title, or a law's penalty amount |

### 2.2 Why Hallucination Is Especially Dangerous in an Office Context

In professional administration, errors in:
- **Legal documents** → contractual liability
- **Regulatory filings** → penalties, rejection, or legal non-compliance
- **Financial reports** → management misinformation, audit failures
- **HR documents** → wrongful action against employees

### 2.3 Verification Protocol for AI Outputs

Every AI-generated output used for official business must pass through a **verification protocol (protokol pengesahan)**:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Identify all claims** in the AI output that are factual (names, numbers, dates, legal citations, statistics) |
| 2 | **Cross-check** each claim against an authorised source (legislation text, official report, contract, database) |
| 3 | **Correct or remove** any unverifiable claim — do not assume it is correct because it sounds plausible |
| 4 | **Log the verification** — note what was checked and by whom, for audit purposes |
| 5 | **Sign off** — the human reviewer approves the corrected document and takes accountability for its content |

## 3. AI Bias in HR and Administrative Contexts (Berat Sebelah AI)

### 3.1 Types of Bias Relevant to Office AI

**Bias (berat sebelah)** in AI systems occurs when the model produces outputs that systematically favour or disadvantage certain groups. Bias can arise from:

| Bias Type | How It Occurs | Office Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Training data bias** | The AI was trained on historical data that reflects past discrimination | AI resume screener trained on historical hires that skewed male continues to rank male candidates higher |
| **Proxy variable bias** | The AI uses a neutral variable (e.g., graduation year, postcode) that correlates with a protected characteristic | AI tool uses postcode to predict employee performance, disadvantaging candidates from lower-income areas |
| **Feedback loop bias** | Human decisions influenced by biased AI output are used to retrain the same AI, reinforcing the bias | AI grading tool scores certain writing styles lower; grader accepts scores; AI retrained to perpetuate the pattern |

### 3.2 Malaysian Context — Protected Characteristics

Under Malaysian law, employment discrimination on grounds of gender, religion, race, and disability is addressed through various statutes. AI tools that produce discriminatory outcomes may expose organisations to legal risk even if the AI decision was "unintentional."

Trainees should note that Malaysia's employment landscape involves multilingual and multicultural considerations. AI tools trained on predominantly English-language data may perform less well on Bahasa Malaysia or Chinese-language inputs, creating potential bias against non-English-writing candidates.

## 4. Intellectual Property Considerations for AI-Generated Content (Harta Intelek)

### 4.1 Who Owns AI-Generated Content?

When AI generates a document, image, or piece of text, the question of **copyright ownership** is complex and evolving:

| Scenario | IP Consideration |
|---|---|
| **AI generates a draft letter** | In most jurisdictions, copyright requires human authorship — a fully AI-generated work with no human creative input may not attract copyright protection. The human who significantly edits and finalises the output may hold copyright in the edited version. |
| **AI generates an image** | Same authorship question applies. Platforms like Midjourney have specific terms on IP ownership — review the tool's Terms of Service. |
| **AI is used to process competitor materials** | Using competitor copyrighted content as AI input (e.g., uploading a competitor's product brochure to train a custom AI) raises potential copyright infringement issues. |
| **AI generates content that resembles existing copyrighted work** | The organisation publishing the content may face infringement claims if AI output closely reproduces protected works. |

**Practical rule:** Always treat AI-generated content as a **first draft** that requires human review, editing, and sign-off. The human creative contribution to the final output strengthens copyright ownership and reduces risk.

### 4.2 Trade Secrets and Confidential Information

Uploading **proprietary business information** (trade secrets, unpublished financial data, client contracts, R&D plans) to a public AI tool may constitute a disclosure of confidential information. This creates two risks:

1. **Loss of trade secret protection** — trade secrets require reasonable steps to maintain secrecy; uploading to a public AI tool may be deemed a breach of confidentiality
2. **Contractual liability** — non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) with clients or partners may prohibit disclosure of their information to third-party platforms, including AI tools

**Rule:** Confidential information and trade secrets must never be uploaded to public AI tools without a valid data processing agreement.

## 5. AI Usage Policy — Core Components (Komponen Utama Polisi Penggunaan AI)

An **AI Usage Policy (Polisi Penggunaan AI)** is a written document that establishes the rules for how employees may and may not use AI tools within the organisation. It is a cornerstone of workplace AI governance.

### 5.1 Required Components of an AI Usage Policy

| Component | Content |
|---|---|
| **Policy Purpose and Scope** | States the purpose of the policy and who it applies to (all employees, contractors, part-time staff) |
| **Approved AI Tools List** | Lists specifically approved AI tools and the business purposes for which each is approved |
| **Prohibited Uses** | Explicit list of banned behaviours (see 5.2 below) |
| **Data Handling Rules** | Specifies what categories of data may and may not be entered into AI tools; references PDPA 2010 compliance |
| **Verification Requirement** | Requires human review and verification of all AI outputs before use in official documents |
| **Intellectual Property** | States that AI-generated content must be reviewed for IP issues before publication |
| **Confidentiality** | Prohibits uploading of confidential business information, client data, or trade secrets to public AI tools |
| **Consequences of Breach** | States the disciplinary action for violation of the policy |
| **Review Schedule** | Specifies how frequently the policy will be reviewed and updated (recommended: annually) |
| **Acknowledgement** | Staff signature block confirming they have read and understood the policy |

### 5.2 Common Prohibited Uses List

An AI Usage Policy should explicitly prohibit:

- Uploading any personal data of clients, employees, or third parties to a public AI tool without a valid Data Processing Agreement
- Publishing AI-generated content externally without human review and approval
- Using AI to generate content intended to deceive, mislead, or manipulate any person
- Representing AI-generated content as the original work of the employee without disclosure
- Using AI tools for personal activities unrelated to work during working hours on company systems
- Using unapproved AI tools for business purposes (shadow AI)
- Using AI to make final decisions on matters with legal, financial, or HR consequences without human review

### 5.3 Governance Structure — AI Committee

Larger organisations should establish an **AI Governance Committee (Jawatankuasa Tadbir Urus AI)** to oversee AI adoption. Recommended composition:

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **AI Governance Lead / DPO** | Overall policy ownership; breach notification; regulatory liaison |
| **IT / Cybersecurity Representative** | Tool security assessment; access controls; incident response |
| **HR Representative** | Staff training; policy communication; disciplinary procedures |
| **Legal / Compliance Representative** | Legal risk assessment; contract review (AI vendor DPAs) |
| **Department Head Representatives** | Practical use case approval; user feedback |

For SMEs, a single designated person may hold multiple roles on this committee.

## 6. Building an Internal AI Ethics Culture (Membina Budaya Etika AI)

An AI ethics culture is not built through a single training session — it requires sustained effort across multiple channels:

| Activity | Frequency | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| AI Policy induction for new staff | Once, on joining | HR |
| Annual AI ethics refresher training | Annually | AI Governance Lead / IT |
| Department-level AI use case review | Quarterly | Department Heads |
| AI incident reporting mechanism | Ongoing | IT / AI Governance Lead |
| Policy review and update | Annually or after any significant AI incident | AI Governance Lead |
| Internal AI ethics newsletter / update | Quarterly | Communications / HR |

An ethics culture requires that employees feel safe reporting AI misuse or concerns without fear of blame. Organisations should establish a **speak-up mechanism** that allows confidential reporting of suspected policy violations.
