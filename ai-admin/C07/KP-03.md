## Objektif Pembelajaran / Learning Objectives

Upon completion of this Information Sheet, trainees will be able to:

1. Identify phishing attacks and explain three indicators of a phishing email or message.
2. Apply best practices for password management including passphrase construction and two-factor authentication (2FA).
3. Explain when and how to use a VPN for remote work.
4. Classify data into four levels (public, internal, confidential, restricted) and apply appropriate handling rules to each level.
5. Execute the four-step data breach incident response procedure: detect, contain, assess, notify.
6. Draft a PDPA breach notification letter to the Personal Data Protection Commissioner.
7. Conduct and document a post-incident review for a data privacy incident.

## 1. Phishing Attack Identification and Prevention (Pengenalan dan Pencegahan Phishing)

### 1.1 What Is a Phishing Attack?

**Phishing (pancingan data)** is a social engineering attack in which a malicious actor disguises themselves as a trusted entity — a bank, a government agency, a colleague, or a senior manager — to trick the target into:

- Revealing login credentials
- Clicking a malicious link or downloading malware
- Transferring funds or sensitive data

Phishing is the **most common** cybersecurity threat facing Malaysian organisations. AI has made phishing attacks more sophisticated — attackers can now use AI to generate personalised, grammatically correct phishing messages in Bahasa Malaysia, English, and Chinese.

### 1.2 Indicators of a Phishing Email or Message

| Indicator | What to Look For | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Suspicious sender address** | Email domain does not match the claimed organisation; uses free email (gmail, yahoo) | `support@maybank-security-alert.com` instead of `@maybank.com` |
| **Urgency and pressure** | Creates time pressure to act immediately without thinking | "Your account will be closed in 24 hours unless you verify now" |
| **Generic greeting** | Does not address you by name | "Dear Valued Customer" instead of your actual name |
| **Mismatched or suspicious link** | Hover over link — actual URL does not match displayed text | Displays `www.cimbclicks.com` but link goes to `www.c1mb.net` |
| **Unexpected attachment** | Invoice, delivery notice, or document you did not expect | "Your parcel is ready — click to download invoice" |
| **Unusual request from known contact** | Asks you to do something the person would not normally ask | CEO WhatsApp asks you to urgently transfer money without prior arrangement |
| **Poor formatting (increasingly rare with AI)** | Spelling errors, inconsistent fonts, awkward phrasing | Less reliable indicator now that AI can write convincing text |

### 1.3 What to Do If You Receive a Suspected Phishing Message

1. **Do not click** any links or download any attachments.
2. **Do not reply** to the message.
3. **Report** to your IT department or designated cybersecurity contact immediately.
4. If you use Microsoft Outlook, use the **"Report Phishing"** button.
5. If the message claims to be from a bank or government agency, call the official number (from the official website — not from the message) to verify.
6. **Delete** the message after reporting.

### 1.4 Business Email Compromise (BEC) — A Phishing Variant

**Business Email Compromise (Penipuan E-mel Perniagaan — BEC)** is a targeted phishing attack where an attacker impersonates a senior executive or trusted supplier to authorise a fraudulent payment or data transfer. BEC attacks cause significant financial losses to Malaysian businesses annually.

Prevention: Establish a **verbal verification protocol** — any email requesting a payment change or data transfer must be verbally confirmed with the requester via a known phone number before action is taken.

## 2. Password Management (Pengurusan Kata Laluan)

### 2.1 The Passphrase Approach

A **passphrase (frasa kata laluan)** is a sequence of random words used as a password. Passphrases are:

- Longer than typical passwords (16+ characters), making them resistant to brute-force attacks
- Easier to remember than random character strings
- Accepted by most systems as a valid password

**Example passphrase:** `Kucing-Hijau-Meja-Tiga-2026!`

This is significantly more secure than `P@ssw0rd123` because length matters more than character complexity for password strength.

### 2.2 Password Best Practices

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Never reuse passwords across systems** | A breach on one platform exposes all other accounts if the same password is used |
| **Use a password manager** | Allows unique, complex passwords for every account without needing to remember them (recommended: Bitwarden, 1Password) |
| **Change passwords after a suspected breach** | Immediate action limits damage from compromised credentials |
| **Never share passwords** | Shared passwords make audit trails impossible |
| **Never write passwords on paper visible in the office** | Physical security is part of cybersecurity |

### 2.3 Two-Factor Authentication (2FA / Pengesahan Dua Faktor)

**Two-factor authentication (2FA)** requires a second verification step beyond the password. It dramatically reduces the risk of unauthorised access even when a password is compromised.

| 2FA Method | Security Level | Practical Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Authenticator App (e.g., Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator)** | HIGH | Generates a time-limited 6-digit code; recommended for all business systems |
| **SMS One-Time Password (OTP)** | MEDIUM | Convenient but can be intercepted via SIM-swap attacks; acceptable for lower-risk systems |
| **Hardware security key (e.g., YubiKey)** | VERY HIGH | Physical device plugged in to authenticate; used for highest-security accounts |
| **Biometric (fingerprint / face)** | HIGH | Built into most modern devices; acceptable for device-level 2FA |

**Rule:** Enable 2FA on all business systems — email, cloud storage, HR platforms, accounting software, and AI tool subscriptions — as a minimum standard.

## 3. VPN Usage for Remote Work (Rangkaian Peribadi Maya untuk Kerja Jauh)

### 3.1 What Is a VPN?

A **Virtual Private Network (VPN / Rangkaian Peribadi Maya)** creates an encrypted tunnel for your internet connection, protecting data transmitted between your device and the internet from interception.

### 3.2 When to Use a VPN

| Situation | Use VPN? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Working on public Wi-Fi (café, hotel, airport) | **YES — mandatory** | Public Wi-Fi is unencrypted; attackers can intercept data |
| Accessing company systems remotely | **YES — mandatory** | Protects login credentials and data in transit |
| Accessing internal company servers or databases | **YES — mandatory** | Internal systems should only be accessible via VPN |
| Working from home on a secured private network | **Recommended** | Adds an extra layer; especially important for sensitive data |
| Browsing general internet on office network | **Not required** | Office network should already have security controls |

### 3.3 VPN in a PDPA Context

When employees process personal data remotely, the organisation has an obligation under the **Security Principle of PDPA 2010** to ensure that data in transit is protected. Mandatory VPN use for remote data processing is a practical implementation of this obligation.

## 4. Data Classification (Klasifikasi Data)

### 4.1 Why Classify Data?

Not all data carries the same risk. A data classification system assigns each category of data a **protection level** and associated handling rules, so that security resources are proportionate to the actual risk.

### 4.2 Four-Level Classification System

| Classification Level | Malay Term | Description | Examples | Handling Rules |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Awam | Intentionally released for public access; no harm from disclosure | Published company brochures, public website content, press releases | No restrictions on handling or sharing |
| **Internal** | Dalaman | Not for public release but not highly sensitive; disclosure causes minor inconvenience | Internal memos, staff directories, project schedules, meeting agendas | Share within organisation only; no external sharing without approval |
| **Confidential** | Sulit | Sensitive business or personal information; disclosure could cause significant harm | Client contracts, financial reports, employee salary data, PDPA-protected personal data | Access restricted to authorised personnel; encrypted in storage and transit; must not be entered into public AI tools |
| **Restricted** | Terhad | Highest sensitivity; disclosure could cause severe harm, legal liability, or regulatory breach | Client PII databases, trade secrets, strategic business plans, legal case files, medical records | Strictest access controls; encryption mandatory; physical security measures; access logged; never transmitted outside secure channels |

### 4.3 Applying Classification to AI Tool Use

| Data Level | May It Be Entered Into a Public AI Tool? |
|---|---|
| Public | Yes — no restriction |
| Internal | Only anonymised or non-identifying internal content; caution required |
| Confidential | **NO** — requires enterprise AI tool with Data Processing Agreement |
| Restricted | **NEVER** — must not leave the organisation's secure systems without explicit legal authorisation |

## 5. Data Breach Incident Response (Prosedur Tindak Balas Insiden Pelanggaran Data)

### 5.1 What Is a Data Breach?

A **data breach (pelanggaran data)** is any incident that results in the unauthorised access to, disclosure of, loss of, alteration of, or destruction of personal data. Under PDPA 2010 (as amended), a data breach that is likely to result in significant harm to data subjects triggers a **mandatory notification obligation**.

Common causes of data breaches in the office:

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| Phishing | Employee clicks malicious link; attacker gains access to email account containing customer data |
| Misconfigured cloud storage | Shared drive folder set to "public" accidentally exposes client files |
| Lost or stolen device | Laptop without encryption is stolen; contains unencrypted customer database |
| Accidental upload to public AI tool | Employee uploads client list to free AI tool to summarise — data now processed by third party without consent |
| Insider threat | Disgruntled employee downloads and leaks customer data |
| Software vulnerability | Outdated system with unpatched vulnerability is exploited |

### 5.2 The Four-Step Incident Response Procedure

#### Step 1 — DETECT (Kesan)

The breach is identified. Detection may come from:
- An IT alert or security monitoring system
- An employee reporting suspicious activity
- A data subject reporting that their data appears to have been compromised
- A notification from an external party (e.g., another organisation, law enforcement)

**Immediate action:** The employee who discovers the breach must **immediately report** it to the designated IT contact or AI Governance Lead / DPO. Do not attempt to resolve the breach independently.

**Document:** Record the date and time the breach was detected, who detected it, and the initial description of what happened.

#### Step 2 — CONTAIN (Kurungan)

Stop the breach from spreading or worsening:

| Action | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Revoke access credentials of affected accounts | Prevent attacker from continuing to access systems |
| Isolate affected devices from the network | Prevent malware from spreading |
| Disable the misconfigured sharing link or cloud setting | Stop further unauthorised access |
| Preserve evidence — do not immediately delete logs | Logs are needed for assessment and regulatory investigation |
| Notify internal stakeholders (management, IT, legal) | Coordinate response |

#### Step 3 — ASSESS (Penilaian)

Determine the scope and severity of the breach:

| Assessment Question | Purpose |
|---|---|
| What personal data was involved? (names, IC, financial, health?) | Determine sensitivity and regulatory obligation |
| How many data subjects are affected? | Scale of potential harm |
| What is the likely consequence for affected individuals? (financial fraud, identity theft, embarrassment?) | Determine if notification to data subjects is required |
| Was the breach intentional or accidental? | Influences investigation and remediation approach |
| Has the breach been fully contained? | Confirm no ongoing exposure |

#### Step 4 — NOTIFY (Beritahu)

Under the PDPA 2010 amendments (Act A1727), where a personal data breach is likely to affect the rights and freedoms of data subjects, the data controller must:

1. **Notify the Personal Data Protection Commissioner** (Pesuruhjaya Perlindungan Data Peribadi / JPDP)
2. **Notify affected data subjects** where the breach is likely to result in significant harm to them

> **NOTA PENTING — TIMEFRAME:** The statutory requirement under Act A1727 is that notification to the Commissioner must be made **"as soon as practicable"** after the data controller becomes aware of the breach. Where the breach is likely to cause significant harm to data subjects, notification to those individuals must be made **"without unnecessary delay"**. The 72-hour deadline that circulated widely appeared only in the public consultation paper and **was not enacted** in the final legislation. The penalty for failing to notify the Commissioner is a fine **not exceeding RM250,000** and/or imprisonment **not exceeding 2 years**. **Trainees must check pdp.gov.my for the operative guideline and any subsidiary regulations** — do not rely on secondary sources for the precise notification requirements.

### 5.3 Contents of a Breach Notification to the Commissioner

A breach notification letter to the Personal Data Protection Commissioner should contain:

| Required Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Identity of the data controller | Organisation name, registration number, address, contact person |
| Date and time breach was detected | When the organisation first became aware |
| Nature of the breach | What happened (e.g., phishing, accidental upload, device theft) |
| Description of personal data affected | Categories of data (names, IC, email, health data, etc.) and approximate number of records |
| Estimated number of data subjects affected | Approximate figure; confirm as more information becomes available |
| Likely consequences | Potential harm to data subjects |
| Measures taken or proposed | Steps to contain and remediate the breach |
| Contact details of DPO or responsible officer | For the Commissioner's follow-up |

### 5.4 Post-Incident Review (Semakan Selepas Insiden)

After the breach is resolved, the organisation must conduct a **post-incident review** to:

1. Document the full timeline of the incident (from initial breach to resolution)
2. Identify the root cause
3. Assess the effectiveness of the response
4. Identify what improvements are needed (technical, process, training)
5. Update the AI Usage Policy and data security procedures accordingly
6. Brief relevant staff on lessons learned
7. File the post-incident report and store it securely for future audit reference

## 6. Practical Guidance — "What If" Scenarios for Office Staff

| Scenario | Correct Action |
|---|---|
| You receive an email from "LHDN" asking you to click a link to verify your company's tax account | Do NOT click. Report to IT. Call LHDN's official number to verify. |
| Your colleague accidentally shares a folder containing client IC copies with "Anyone with the link" on Google Drive | Remove sharing immediately. Report to IT and DPO. Assess how long the link was active. File a breach report if link was accessed. |
| You are working from a hotel and need to access the company HR system to update employee records | Connect via company VPN first. Only access the system after VPN is confirmed active. |
| The CEO's WhatsApp sends you a message asking you to buy RM2,000 in gift vouchers urgently | Call the CEO's known phone number to verify. BEC attacks often impersonate senior management. Do not transfer money or purchase vouchers without verbal confirmation. |
| An employee leaves the company and their access to all systems has not been revoked | Immediately report to IT. All departing employee accounts must be deactivated on or before their last day. |
