1. Overview 
1.1 Event Coordinator 
Also known as  event specialist. This person is taking charge of the planning and running of 
every facet of events .  Generally , the coordinator is to make sure that the event runs 
smoothly, efficiently and could handle any crisis that may come up. Event coordinator 
controls an event from conception to clean up. 
An event coordinator requires to posses the following norms and abilities. 
i. Communication Ability 
The ability to discuss and listen to clients’ needs and presenting ideas and solutions to 
their requirements. A coordinator also works with suppliers, contractors, staff and many 
other parties. Customer service, linguistic skills and interpersonal skills are vital. 
ii. Calm and Dependability 
He is capable to work under pressure.  Many things can go wrong during an event.  The 
coordinators needs to remain calm and steady during such an incident and en sure the 
event progresses well. 
iii. Computer Skills 
The current event planners rely on computers to communicate, plan, budget  
preparation and organize their events. 
iv. Negotiation Skills  
He is capa ble to discuss and negotiate on prices and schedules with clients and 
contractors. 
v. Organizing Skills 
Many things can happen during planning and in meetings. The coordinators need to 
stay focused and manage to structure the events so that it goes on without a hitch. 
vi. Problem Solving Skills 
Inevitably, anything may go wrong at an event. The coordinator should be able to 
quickly manage and resolve the issue.

4.2 The Event Industry 
There are several loosely -defined terms in the event industry. That is, words that can be 
interpreted in different ways. The word  ‘event’ by itself could mean anything from a birthday 
party to benefit concert. The point is, we all speak within our own context, but sometimes you 
need to take a step back to make sure everyone understands exactly what is being said. 
4.3 Event Planning 
When it comes to event planning careers, some people outside the industry may get 
confused at the difference between an event planner and an event coordinator. 
Generally speaking, the planner makes the critical decisions regarding the what, when, 
where, who and how. An event coordinator on the other hand, is responsible for making sure 
all the details are executed, and that each vendor shows up on time and performs 
appropriately. 
4.4 Event Coordinator 
Larger organizations may have two coordinators for every planner, while smaller operations 
might only have one coordinator that serves the needs of all executive decision makers. 
Either way there is typically a direct line of supervision ov er the coordinator position. This 
does not mean that the event coordinators have  it easy. Most of them have difficult task of 
organizing everything on -site with only a few hours before the event begins. This , of course 
is when most problems occur, which is  why a good coordinator is focused, organized and 
resourceful. 
At the core of any event -related organization is the event coordinator. This is the person 
responsible for supervising and executing all of the details from setup to tear down. 
Event coordinato rs are typically more hands -on than planners and administrators. Their 
primary role is to oversee the execution of details provided by the lead planner. Thus, it is the 
lead planner who makes the final decision regarding venue selection, meal choices and 
itinerary times, while the event coordinator insures everything goes as planned. 
Depending on the company or organization, an event coordinator may have other 
responsibilities. Here is an overview of specific duties that accompany coordinator positions 
across various industries. 
i. Corporations 
In a corporate environment, the coordinator reports directly to a lead planner or 
executive decision maker. The coordinator becomes the direct contact between 
the event vendors (hotels, caterer, etc.) and the supervisor.  His job is to

Event coordinators for hotel and venu es report to the banquet director or sales

Large non -profit organisation such as Red Cr escent Society and other NGOs hold 
plenty of events every now and then, because they serve so many different groups. The 
number of events , combined with limited bud get, means non -profit event coordinators 
will be asked to perform a wider range of duties. This can include things like organizing 
volunteers, participating in fundraising  and soliciting material donations.  Non-profit 
careers are not for everyone, and it is important to be passionate with their cause 
because much of the works are indeed a labour of love. 
v. Independent Planners 
Event planners that become successful running their  own business  will eve ntually 
expand their operations. Their first hire will likely be an assistant coordin ator.  They are 
not only with the event coordinator and expected to work with clients and vendors, but 
they will also have to serve the needs of the business owner as well. That means 
handling some of the more time consuming tasks that will keep the owner in growing 
his/her business further. On the bright side, working for an independent planner offers 
an unprecedented opportunity to learn as the coordinator and will be immersed in all 
facets of the business.

i. Choosing the Event 
To see which of the se two directions you are heading for, consider its difficulty.  Some 
people or  organizations may endure painful debates about the purpose of an event. 
They say not everyone would attend the event because of other constraints, such as 
too high ticket prices. But if your principal goal is to raise fund, you will have to

having more people to attend it at a lower price may help you achieve the goal.  If your 
targeted audience for the event is successful business man who wants to show support 
for this population and who also has the means of giving notably to the cause. 
ii. Types of Event

 more donor prospect

financial return in short run. They have the following characteristics; 
 low individual cost to participate 
 larger attendance / higher visibility 
 higher administrative and advertising cost 
 fewer donor prospects 
 less loyalty to the organization on the part of those who participate

v. Performance Result Evaluation 
You ar e just being nominated to coordinate a  company’s event. This is a 
great opportunity for you to show your skills, and potentially earn a rise in process. 
There is only one problem; you never been exposed to such planning for an event 
before. Not to worry. Plenty of others have found themselves in the same 
predicament, and you will succeed because you are organized and determined. 
Just like any  other event under taking , firstly you need to organize all the 
information you have and then identify the most urgent matters. 
Before choosing a venue or hiring a speaker , you need to know the basi c 
parameters of your events. It is almost impossible to talk details and costs with the 
third-party until you finalise your plan. 
Here are key details you should define before contacting your potential vendors. 
i. Date 
Everything revolves around the date of your event. Ideally you want to have 
three potential dates in mind so that you can compare availability and prices 
across the board. Try to include different days of the  week for maximum 
flexibility. 
ii. Time Frame 
One single hour difference can make an impact on your planning, so you 
really need to be specific about your anticipated time frames. For example, 
starting at 10am instead of 8am means you may not need to allocate a 
breakfast budget. 
 
 
iii. Attendance Numbers 
This is one detail that is often too broadly estimated for productive negotiations.  
Get realistic with your attendance projections. Discuss in advance who you will 
invite and put your projections together on a spreadsheet. Attendance and 
budget are directly correlated, so don’t haphazardly guess about the number of 
people you think will attend.

iv. Breakout Sessions 
You may onl y need one banquet room if you are hosting a dinner, but a full 
scale conference will likely require additional breakout rooms and an exhibition 
space. Once again, you won’t be able to measure costs until you define how 
much space you need. For conferences , the best approach is to plan a mock 
itinerary in your very first meeting. Sharing ideas and piecing them together on 
paper will help steer you through the next planning decisions. 
v. Budget vs. Costs 
It might sound impractical to work on a budget before kno wing venue 
and catering costs , but working this way provides a benchmark for you to 
measure all of vendor proposals. Begin with your revenue sources. 
a. How much should you charge for attendance? 
b. Will you call on sponsors to help offset costs? 
c. What will exhibitors pay to participate? 
d. Revenues will ultimately dictate your budget, so it only makes sense 
to project them first. 
vi. Marketing 
After you have identifie d your revenue sources, the next step is to outline 
your marketing plan. What is the best way to reach and engage your revenue 
generators? Perhaps you have a list of potential atte ndees as an employer or 
membership association. That would obviously be helpful, but what about 
sponsors and exhibitors? If you are planning to attract more exhibitors then 
you must be able to reach them. Targeted marketing campaigns cost money, 
and that cuts into your revenues. 
vii. Transportation and Lodging 
Running a local conference typically eliminates any concerns about 
transportation. However, you still need to address lodging for speakers, 
exhibitors and special guests. Some hotels provide discounts on meeting 
space if you book enough room nights. The only way to capitalize on this is by 
knowing your lodging needs in advance. Go back through your attendance 
data and project how many guests are from outside your area. 
Now all of this probably sounds like a lot of information to  gather up front, and 
it is. But keep in mind that these are the details that affect everything else in 
the planning process. You might be i nitially attracted to the more ‘ glamorous’ 
tasks, like site visits and menu planning. Just remember that their time will

i. Type A generally raises more collections, but less public awareness. This case 
they have the following characteristics; 
 high individual cost to participate 
 smaller attendance 
 lower administrative and advertising investment 
 more donor prospect 
 greater loyalty to the organization on the part of those attending 
ii. Type B events normally succeed at raising the organization’s visibility, but they 
often require a bigger investment of t ime and produce a smaller financial return 
in short run. They have the following characteristics; 
 low individual cost to participate 
 larger attendance / higher visibility 
 higher administrative and advertising cost 
 fewer donor prospects 
 less loyalty to the organization on the part of those who participate
