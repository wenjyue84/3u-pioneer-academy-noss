1. Overview 
Relationship management is a  strategy employed by an organization in which a continuous 
level of engagement is maintained between the organization and its audience.  It is a focus of 
the financial and investing industries as  a way to identify potential cross -sales of products 
and services. 
The relationship management can be between a business and its customers or between a 
business and other businesses . As with any other type of important business relationship, 
the intent of managing how a supplier performs is to ensure the highest possible quality of 
goods; on time, and within budget. 
 1.1 Supplier Relationship Management (SRM) 
This term is used to describe a process by which a business interacts and conducts 
business with a company that furnishes goods that are either products or services. 
i) Customer Relationship Management (CRM) 
It describes the strategy that a company uses to handle customer 
interactions. One ex ample of a common CRM strategy is the  rewards 
card program offered by supermarkets. The store gives its customers a 
free card with certain benefits when they swipe the card dur ing 
checkout. In return, the supermarket tracks everything the customer 
buys and allows the store to create an extremely detailed customer 
profile based on his or her purchasing habits. 
 
  ii) Client Relationship Management 
It is a mean to develop good rapport between a client and the supplier or 
service provider that will reduce tense relationship between them.  It 
helps to reduce the tension and build confidence in clients thrust.  
Tension arises with clients when expectations are not met in the eyes of 
the client.

2. Enhance Interpersonal Conflict Resolution 
Whenever there are two or more people come together, there is tendency to be in conflict.  In 
conflict resolutions, you will learn crucial conflict management skill, including dealing with 
anger and u sing the agreement frame.  Dealing with conflict is important for every 
organization irrespective of its size. If it is left unattended or not being properly resolved, it 
can lead to production lost, absenteeism, wear and tear and even law suits. 
Conflict is complicated subject. Not many people are possibly remember a long steps of 
things to do and not to do during a conflict conversation. So, in this short lesson we try to 
focus our energy and attention on places most likelihood of shifting the conflict to  a better 
position. 
In interpersonal conflict situations involving relationships that you want or need to continue, 
the followings are usually the primary goals for everyone involved. There may be some other 
points, but these are the ones people return to again and again when mediating, coaching or 
transforming a workplace or personal conflict. 
- Alleviate ongoing tension so that the personal relationship is more joyful or the 
professional relationship returns to be productive. 
- Handle yourself in a way you ca n feel good about, so that no further damage on the 
relationship and, if at work, fosters your career. 
- See if you can sort out the primary dispute and, if not, figure out how to move for ward 
without resolution. In order to achieve these goals, follow the three outlined below as 
the interpersonal conflict fundamentals. 
 
 2.2 Manage Your Own Response 
The only person you can truly manage during conflict is yourself.  During conflict 
conversations, many people focus attention on the other’s behavior, on the other’s 
transgressions, and on what they demand the other person start or stop doing. So let’s  
stop doing what isn’t working and disempowering yourself in the process. When you 
make the fix to a conflict entirely the other person’s job, you hand all power to them for 
how the future unfolds. 
 The way you act in conflict is heavily influenced by the way you think about conflict 
and its resolution s, by the narrative you tell yourself and others about the situation. By 
choosing a certain kind of narrative, you create opportunity to change the conflict and 
its impact on the relationship. The narrative you choose to tell yourself and others about 
the conflict will determine your words and actions.

What does it mean to manage your own response and choose the right kind of 
narrative?  Use these guidelines to figure out what kind of response will serve you best. 
i) Work to understand what it is about the other person’s behavior that is 
getting under your skin. Understanding that will give you the key to 
neutralizing your ‘inner lizard’. 
ii) Identify ‘stoppers’, quick mechanisms to help you stop doing something 
you know won’t serve you well but is still a habit that’s hard to break. 
Stoppers are a mental shortcut to help you bridge the gap between 
what you know and what you actually do. 
iii) Notice the narratives you tell yourself and others about this conflict. Is it 
helping you or blinding you to a fuller understanding of the problem? 
Stop blaming.  It wastes energy, escalates conflict, sidetracks the  
conversation and achieves l ittle beyond feeding your inner lizard’s bad 
behavior. 
Note 
Just remember this when talking about personal conflict issue , “Every person 
you fight with has many other people in his life with whom he gets along quite 
well. You cannot look at a person who se ems difficult to you without also 
looking at yourself”. – Jeffrey Kottler, psychologist 
 
 2.3  Do your part to make collaborative problem solving feasible 
When there is an ongoing period of tension, every little transgression registers, just like a 
minor scrape on a scab can start it bleeding again. Relationships that took a long while to 
become tense and unhappy will take a while to heal. One of your primary tasks is to create 
space for that to occur and to protect the ‘between’ from further damage. 
Here are some effective ways for protecting the relationship the best you can from 
increased tension while you work to sort out the problems that caused it. 
i) If a conversation gets tense or begins to go downhill in another way, stop until you 
have your balance back. Proceed  when you  have lost your cool serves no one 
well. 
ii) Use door openers  to initi ate conversation about the tension, to invite the other 
person to understand it with you. Do  not focus on fixing first ; focus on 
understanding first. 
iii) When you do have to confront a problem, do your part to increase the odds the 
other person will want to stay in conversation with you. The other person(s) can

As noted above, when things are tense it can seem like every move by you or the other

3. Promote Empathy 
 
 3.1  What is Empathy 
Empathy is the capacity to understand or feel what another person is experiencing from 
within the other person's frame of reference; that is the capacity to place oneself in 
another's shoes. 
Compassion and sympathy are two terms that many associate with empathy, but all 
three of these terms are unique. Compassion is an emotion we feel when others are in 
need, which motivates us to help them. Sympathy is a feeling of care and 
understanding for someone in need. 
Empathy is distinct from  sympathy, pity, and  emotional contagion . Sympathy 
or empathic concern  is the feeling of  compassion or concern for another, the wish to 
see them better off or happier. Pity is feeling that another is in trouble and in need of 
help as they cannot fix their problems themselves, often described as ‘feeling sorry’ for 
someone. Emotional contagio n is when a person imitatively catches the emotions that 
others are showing without necessarily recognizing this is happening.  
 
Empathy leads to sympathy by definition unlike the over -aroused emotional response 
that turns into personal distress and causes a turning-away from another’s distress. 
In empathy we feel what we believe are the emotions of another, which makes it both 
affective and cognitive by most psychologists.   In this sense, arousal and empathy 
promote pro-social behavior as we accommodate ea ch other to feel similar emotions. 
Measures of empathy show that mirror neurons are activated during arousal of 
sympathetic responses and prolonged activation shows increased probability to help 
others. 
 
 3.2 Components of Empathy 
Empathy can be divided into two major components. 
i) Affective Empathy 
Also known as emotional empathy is  the capacity to respond with an 
appropriate emotion to another's mental states.  The ability to empathize 
emotionally is supposed to be based on emotional contagion being affe cted by 
another's emotional or arousal state.  
Affective empathy can be subdivided into the following scales: 
 Empathic Concern

4. Provide Motivation 
Motivation is an employee's intrinsic enthusiasm about and drive to accomp lish 
activities related to work. Motivation is that internal drive that causes an individual to 
decide on action. 
An individual's  motivation is influenced by biological, intell ectual, social and emotional 
factors. As such, motivation is a complex, not easily defined, intrinsic driving force that 
can also be influenced by external factors. 
 
 4.1 Three Motivation Theories 
In general, currently most organizational management make u se of three motivational 
theories. 
) Cognitive Dissonance Theory 
This theory involves being motivated to make a decision of some kind based on 
the fact that doing one thing or being a certain way feels uncomfortable.  People 
do not like to feel uncomfortab le.  They hate the feelings of dissonance.  If a 
person feels that he stands out for some reasons, it makes him feel awkward.  In 
order to reduce the stress of that situation, that person is motivated to formulate

styles or even intentionally getting his performance or life styles changed to better 
fit in.   
ii) Endowed Progress Effect 
In real life, any perception of progress is motivating in itself.  If a person perceives 
that he is making progress toward a goal he set, he is motivated to continue 
working at it.  Conversely, if no progress is perceived, of course he is likely to 
abandon his efforts. Take an example in teaching sector. Students could be 
motivated during tes ts or assignment slots. Say, for certain numbers of test or 
assignment questions, just simply throw in a ridiculously easy question.  The 
student has no problem in answering it, they feel affirmed in their efforts, they see 
a quickly completed problem, and they feel motivated to continue working on it.   
iii)   Extrinsic Motivation 
People are motivated to do something because there are some kinds of tangible 
reward at the end of it. Most people do it for the reward. Nevertheless, there are 
cases whereby peo ple do it for fun.  A person could say that he does something 
because he enjoys doing it.  That is not extrinsic motivation.  People go to work 
because they get paid.  Doing something because you get rewarded at the end of 
it is extrinsic.  There is a tang ible reward and it works on all kinds of people.  It 
works really well with kids.  Extrinsic motivation should be on continuous effort so 
that it w ould not becoming defaulted.  Trained animals get motivated as they get 
rewarded (food) at the end of their acts is extrinsic motivation. 
 
4.2  Motivation Without Monetary Rewards 
There are eight ways a leader can motivate employees without monetary rewards.   
Most successful entrepreneurs will tell you that their primary motivation is to ‘change 
the world’ and to build something lasting, not to make a lot of money. The conventional 
wisdom is that employees work for money above anything else. 
Some people believe that non -cash motivators may be more effective in the long term 
than financial incentives.   There are pe ople who believe that motivation is strongly 
tailored to leadership.  The employees value having strong leaders, who incent them to 
do their best, just as much if not more than monetary rewards.  For action we need to 
be trained from what we rebuild employ ees’ morale following tough economic times, 
with limited budgets: 
i)  Energize Your Team

Instead of being the type of leader who sucks the energy away from others, 
resolve to be the kind of leader who strives to bring passion and positive 
energy at workplace. Your employees have just helped you pull the company 
through one bad economic period. It is time for them to have a source of 
positive energy. 
ii)  More to Life Than Work 
Great leaders have deep reserves of physical, spiritual, and emotional energy, 
and that energy is usually fuelled by a strong and supportive relationship with 
the people they love, regular exercise, a healthy lifestyle, and setting aside time 
for reflection. 
 iii) Priority to Employees 
No organization is better than the people who run it. The fact is that you are in 
the people business; hiring, training, and managing people to deliver the 
product or service you provide. If the people are the engine of your success, to 
be a great leader you need to attend to your people with a laser-like focus. 
iv) Act With Integrity 
When there are reports publicly exposing the stories of leaders who have acted 
inappropriately and have gone against the best interests of the employees, 
showing your employees that you value integrity can help motivate them and 
create a sense of pride for your organization. 
v) Be a Great Communicator 
Leadership is influencing others. It cannot be achieved without effective 
communication. If you are struggling with communicating to your employees, 
first work on your ability to  influence individuals by choosing words that are 
impactful to carry your message. Then you need to figure out how to 
communicate to a larger audience. 
vi) Be a Great Listener 
The most effective leaders are those who can spare their time to listen.  They 
are not merely just listening to their team members’ words but also to the 
priceless hidden meaning between the lines. Remember that during good times 
and bad, sometimes the employees just need someone to talk to. 
Communicate to them that you are always waiting with open ears. 
 vii) Be a Problem Solver

5. Demonstrate Initiative Behavior 
Behaviour is what humans do. It is observable and measurable. Whether it is walk from one 
place to another or to crack our knuckles, behaviour serves some functions or the other. 
Applied Behaviour Analysis, the research based approach to modifying behaviour, seeks to find 
the function of an inappropriate behaviour in order to find a replacement behaviour to replace it. 
Every behaviour serves some functions, and provides a consequence  or reinforcement for the 
behaviour. 
 
Reinforcement is the means by which behavior is increased and also known as consequences.  
Positive reinforcement  adds something that will make it more likely the behavior will occur. 
Negative reinforcement is when something is removed and it is more likely to continue.

5.2 Management Behavior Solution 
 i)  The Risk Management Process 
Risk Management is defined as a systematic application of management policies, 
procedures and practices to the  tasks of establishing the context, identifying, 
analysing, assessing, treating, monitoring and communicating.  Risk management can 
be applied to all levels of an organisation, in both the strategic and operational 
contexts, to specific projects, decisions and recognised risk areas. 
It is an iterative process that, with each cycle, can contribute progressively to 
organisational improvement by providing management with a greater insight into risks 
and their impact. 
Risk is defined as the chance of something to happen that wi ll have an impact on 
objectives. It is, therefore, important to understand what the objectives of  an 
organisation, departments, sections and work unit or your position  are prior to 
attempting to analyse the risks. 
Risk analysis is best don e in a group with each member of the group having a good 
understanding of the tasks and objectives of the area being analysed. 
 ii) Identify the Risks 
As a group, list the things that might inhibit your ability to meet your objectives. You 
can even look at  the things that would actually enhance your ability to meet those 
objectives eg. a fund -raising commercial opportunity. These are the risks that you 
face eg. loss of a key team member; prolonged IT network outage, delayed provision 
of important information by another work unit/individual, failure to seize a commercial 
opportunity etc. 
iii) Identify the Causes 
Try to identify what might cause these things to occur eg. the key team member 
might be disillusioned with his/her position, might be head hunted to go elsewhere; 
the person upon whom you are relying for information might be very busy, going 
on leave or notoriously slow in supplying such data; the supervisor required to 
approve the commercial undertaking might be risk averse and need extra 
convincing before taking the risk etc. 
iv) Identify the Controls 
Identify all the things ( controls) that you have in place that are aimed at reducing 
the likelihood of your risks from happening in the first place and, if they do happen, 
what you have in place to reduc e their impact ( consequence) eg. providing a

reliance on one person; stress the need for the required information to be supplied 
in a timely manner; send a reminder before t he deadline; provide additional 
information to the supervisor before he/she asks for it etc. 
v) Establish Your Likelihood and Consequence Descriptors 
Remembering that these depend upon the context of your analysis ie. if your 
analysis relates to your work unit, any financial loss or loss of a key staff member, 
for example, will have a greater impact on that work unit than it will have on the 
organisation as a whole so those descriptors used for the whole -of-the

or other work unit or the individual eg. a loss of RM 1.2 millions might be 
considered insignificant to the company, but it could very well be catastrophic to 
your work unit.   This parameters need to be established in consultation with the 
head of work unit. 
 vi) Establish Your Risk Rating Descriptors 
What is meant by a Low, Moderate, High or Extre me Risk needs to be decided 
ahead of time. Because these are more generic in terminology though, you might 
find that the company's Strategic Risk Rating Descriptors are applicable. 
 vii) Add Other Controls 
 Generally speaking, any risk that is rated as High or Extreme should have 
additional controls applied to it in order to reduce it to an acceptable level. What 
the appropriate additional controls might be, whether they can be afforded, what 
priority might be placed on them etc is something for the group to determine in 
consultation with the Head of the work unit , who ideally, should be a member of 
the group doing the analysis in the first place. 
 
 
viii) Make a Decision 
Once the above process is completed, if there are still some risks that are rated as 
High or Extreme, a decision has to be made as to whether the activity will go 
ahead. There will be occasions when the risks are  higher than preferred but there 
may be nothing more that can be done to mitigate that risk ie. they are out of the 
control of the work unit but the activity must still be carried out. In such situations, 
monitoring the circumstances and regular review is essential.
