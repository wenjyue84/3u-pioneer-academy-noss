1. Leadership tasks and responsibilities 
 
Effective performance management process that supports employee performance, 
development and success can only be achieved by involving everyone in the 
organization. Employees that being engaged and informed properly will ultimately 
produce result and higher participation rates plus better quality performance 
management. Information, instruction and job description must be clearly 
communicated to everyone.  Here are the breakdown of task and responsibilities of 
each stakeholder group:  
 
Executives and leaders, managers, employees and human resource executive or 
senior leader have to make sure that you are supportive of the process, communicate 
its value and engage others, you need to be an enthusiastic champion of the new 
process for others to participate. Sharing your support through a variety of employee 
communications vehicles are important and always be prepared to answer questions 
from various groups about “what's in it for me” to help build organizational 
commitment to the task and process. 
 
1.1  
Managers’ tasks and responsibilities  
Managers should play the role and have a responsibility to recognize and 
reinforce strong performance in your employees, identify and encourage 
improvement where needed. Viewing performance tasks and responsibilities 
in management as a two-way discussion that must go on throughout the 
year. Your employees should never be surprised by the ratings and 
feedback they receive in their formal performance reviews. Managers are 
expected to: 
i. 
Use the performance management process as a valuable tool for 
supporting employee development and improvement. 
ii. 
If your employees sense a lack of interest on your part, they'll lose 
interest too.  
iii. 
When talking with your team about the process, be sure to emphasize its 
benefits, and encourage employees to take ownership of their own 
performance and development.

Determine an appropriate schedule for regular performance conversations 
with those you manage directly.  
i. 
Conduct short, regular meetings to discuss and record milestones, 
accomplishments, successes and challenges as they occur, when 
details are fresh in both your minds. This will allow you to better monitor 
progress on goals, and provide coaching as required. Plus, these short 
meetings reduce the effort it takes to prepare for and conduct your 
annual performance reviews because you have tracked progress and 
performance and provided the needed feedback when it was most 
valuable.  
ii. 
Use the annual performance review meeting to review the 
achievements, setbacks, development and training that have already 
been discussed throughout the year and then use this information to 
establish goals and a development plan for the coming year.  
1.3 
Deliver regular, positive and constructive feedback. 
i. 
Give employees feedback during one-on-one meetings and informal as 
regularly as possible. 
ii. 
Commend your employee in front of their peers.  
iii. 
Make performance notes about each employee in the period between 
conversations, so that come conversation time, you have concrete 
examples to share.  
iv. 
Remember that the goal of feedback is to describe desired behaviors 
and expectations, not to dwell on undesirable behaviors.

4. 
Flexibility in work practice 
 
"Flexibility is about an employee and an employer making changes to when, where 
and how a person will work to better meet individual and business needs. Flexibility 
enables both individual and business needs to be met through making changes to 
the time (when), location (where) and manner (how) in which an employee works. 
Flexibility should be mutually beneficial to both the employer and employee and 
result in superior outcomes."  
 
4.1 Formal flexibility  
Formal flexibility are policies that are officially approved human resources 
policies, as well as any official policies that give supervisors discretion to 
provide flexibility."  
 
4.2 Informal flexibility

Informal flexibility refers to "policies that are not official and not written down 
but are still available to some employees, even on a discretionary basis." While 
most formal work arrangements can usually be identified, organizations 
acknowledge that utilization statistics probably under estimate the true reach 
and impact of flexibility, as they cannot accurately determine the extent of 
informal flexibility, for example, employees who occasionally alter their work 
hours or work from home.  
 
Human resources professionals have embraced it and while many are adapting 
their office to accommodate the wants and needs of employees, few really 
know how to put a successful flexible workplace program into place. Some big-
name companies have moved away from flexible procedures, while others 
completely ignore the pursuit of something they view as unattainable. But, 
when correct deployment of workplace flexibility can be the competitive factor 
that attracts, retains the top talent and also can help boost company 
productivity. It just takes time, effort and the occasional trial and error.  
 
Companies of all sizes can learn how to introduce and master a productive, 
flexible work environment in their offices. There are many formulas that work 
seamlessly for employees and management alike, and many others that won’t 
work. Everyone assumes there are pitfalls to going flexible and they're right. 
Overcoming them is possible and can make a company stronger.
