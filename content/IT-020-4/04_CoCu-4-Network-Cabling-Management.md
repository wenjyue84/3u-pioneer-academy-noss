| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Structured cabling standards (TIA/EIA-568, ISO 11801) | International standards that define the design, installation, and testing requirements for structured cabling systems in commercial buildings. TIA/EIA-568 (North American) and ISO 11801 (international) specify cable categories, maximum cable run lengths (90 m horizontal, 10 m total patch/equipment cords), connector types, and testing parameters. Compliance ensures interoperability, performance guarantees, and warranty coverage from cable manufacturers. The administrator must ensure that the cabling design and installation adhere to the applicable standard and that all cable runs are certified to that standard. |
| Cable categories (Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6a, Cat7) | Classifications of twisted-pair copper cable based on performance characteristics. Cat5e supports up to 1 Gbps at 100 MHz. Cat6 supports up to 10 Gbps at 250 MHz for short runs (up to 55 m). Cat6a supports 10 Gbps at 500 MHz for the full 100 m run. Cat7 is a shielded cable supporting 10 Gbps at 600 MHz, used in environments with high electromagnetic interference. The administrator selects the cable category based on current bandwidth requirements, future growth plans, and the standard specified in the project scope. |
| Fibre optic cable (single-mode, multi-mode) | Glass or plastic fibre that transmits data as light pulses, offering much higher bandwidth and longer distances than copper. Single-mode fibre (SMF) uses a narrow core (9 microns) for long-distance transmission (up to 100 km), typically used for campus backbone and WAN links. Multi-mode fibre (MMF) uses a wider core (50 or 62.5 microns) for shorter distances (up to 550 m at 10 Gbps with OM3/OM4), typically used for building backbone and data centre interconnects. The administrator specifies the fibre type, connector (LC, SC, ST), and cable count based on the network design and growth plan. |
| MDF, IDF, and structured cabling topology | The Main Distribution Frame (MDF) is the central point where the external service provider connection, backbone cabling, and core network equipment (core switch, router, firewall) are located. Intermediate Distribution Frames (IDFs) are located on each floor or wing and house floor switches and horizontal cable terminations. The structured cabling topology follows a hierarchical star pattern from MDF to IDFs to individual outlets. The administrator plans MDF/IDF locations considering cable run distances, power availability, cooling, physical security, and future expansion capacity. |
| Cable management (trays, conduits, ladders, J-hooks) | Physical support systems that route, organise, and protect cables throughout the building. Cable trays and ladders provide open support for large cable bundles in ceilings and risers. Conduits (rigid or flexible) protect cables in exposed areas or where building codes require fire-rated enclosures. J-hooks support small cable bundles along walls and ceilings. Proper cable management prevents damage, maintains bend radius, allows airflow, and makes future moves/adds/changes easier. The administrator specifies the cable management method for each route based on cable count, building construction, and fire code requirements. |
| Labelling and documentation system | A consistent naming and labelling scheme applied to every cable, patch panel port, wall outlet, and rack position. Common schemes include room-rack-port format (e.g. R201-IDF2-P12). Labels are printed using a durable labelling machine and applied at both ends of every cable run, on patch panel ports, and on wall outlets. The labelling scheme is documented in a patch schedule that maps each outlet to its corresponding patch panel port and switch port. Accurate labelling is essential for troubleshooting, moves/adds/changes, and preventing accidental disconnections during maintenance. |

| Cabling Tool / Equipment | Description |
|--------------------------|-------------|
| Cable certifier (e.g. Fluke DSX-8000, DSX-5000) | A professional-grade testing instrument that measures cable performance parameters including wire map, length, insertion loss (attenuation), near-end crosstalk (NEXT), return loss, and propagation delay. The certifier compares measured values against the selected standard (Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6a) and produces a pass/fail result for each cable run. Certification reports are required for manufacturer warranty, compliance with the cabling standard, and project acceptance. The administrator ensures every cable run is certified before handover and files the results with the cabling documentation. |
| Punch-down tool (110 / Krone type) | A hand tool used to terminate individual wires into insulation displacement connectors (IDC) on patch panels, keystone jacks, and 66/110 blocks. The tool strips insulation, pushes the wire into the IDC slot, and trims the excess in a single action. 110-type is the most common in TIA/EIA environments; Krone is used in some European and Telco installations. Proper punch-down technique ensures reliable connections -- insufficient force causes intermittent faults, while incorrect wire placement causes wire map failures during certification. |
| OTDR (Optical Time-Domain Reflectometer) | A fibre optic testing instrument that sends light pulses into a fibre strand and analyses the reflected light to measure fibre length, attenuation, splice loss, connector loss, and locate faults (breaks, bends, or bad splices). The OTDR produces a trace graph showing events along the fibre with distance and loss values. Essential for verifying fibre backbone installations, locating faults in existing fibre runs, and producing acceptance test documentation. The administrator or contractor uses the OTDR after fibre installation and splicing to verify the link meets the design specification. |
| Fibre fusion splicer | A precision instrument that permanently joins two fibre optic strands by aligning their cores and fusing them with an electric arc. Fusion splicing produces the lowest loss and most reliable joint compared to mechanical splicing. Used when installing fibre backbone between MDF and IDFs, repairing broken fibre, or extending existing fibre runs. The administrator or certified contractor operates the splicer, verifies splice loss with the OTDR, and protects the splice with a heat-shrink sleeve. Splice loss must be within the standard limit (typically less than 0.1 dB for single-mode). |
| Cable toner and tracer (probe) | A two-part tool used to identify and trace cables in an existing installation. The toner (transmitter) is connected to one end of the cable and sends an audible tone signal. The probe (receiver) is moved along cable bundles, patch panels, or wall outlets to detect the tone and identify the matching cable. Essential during cable upgrades, troubleshooting, and moves/adds/changes in buildings where cables are not labelled or documentation is outdated. The administrator uses the toner before disconnecting or re-terminating any cable to prevent accidental disruption of the wrong circuit. |
| Labelling machine (thermal transfer or laminated) | A handheld or desktop device that prints durable, adhesive labels for cable ends, patch panel ports, wall outlets, and racks. Professional labelling machines (e.g. Brady BMP21, Brother PT-E550W) support multiple label sizes, wrap-around cable labels, and port identification labels. Labels must be resistant to heat, moisture, and abrasion to remain legible over the cable's lifetime. The administrator establishes the labelling format according to the organisational naming convention and ensures that labels are applied at both ends of every cable run during installation and updated during any subsequent changes. |

| Cabling Record / Document | Description |
|---------------------------|-------------|
| Patch schedule | A table or spreadsheet that maps every wall outlet to its corresponding patch panel port and switch port. Includes cable reference number, outlet location (room, desk position), patch panel and port number, switch and port number, VLAN assignment, and status (active, spare, decommissioned). The patch schedule is the primary reference document for moves/adds/changes and troubleshooting. The administrator updates the patch schedule after every change and distributes updated copies to the support team. |
| As-built cabling diagram | A scaled floor plan or building diagram showing the physical routes of all cable runs, MDF/IDF locations, backbone links, conduit paths, and outlet positions. The as-built diagram reflects the actual installed layout, which may differ from the original design due to site conditions. It is produced by the installer or contractor and verified by the administrator during handover. The diagram is used for capacity planning, fault location, and planning future extensions. |
| Cable management software (NetBox, Patch Manager) | A software application used to document, visualise, and manage the structured cabling infrastructure. Records rack layouts, patch panel connections, cable runs, device assignments, and IP allocations. Provides search and reporting functions for capacity planning and change management. The administrator uses the software instead of or in addition to spreadsheets and diagrams, especially in larger installations where manual tracking becomes error-prone. Integration with network monitoring tools allows correlation between physical and logical topology. |
| Change log and version history | A chronological record of all cabling changes including date, description of change (new run, relocation, decommission), affected ports, responsible person, and approval reference. The change log ensures that diagrams and patch schedules remain current and provides an audit trail for troubleshooting and compliance. Version numbering is applied to updated diagrams and patch schedules each time a change is implemented. |


| Common Fault | Cause | Action |
|--------------|-------|--------|
| Cable certification failure (fail on NEXT, return loss, or length) | Cable kinked or bent below minimum bend radius during installation; poor termination (untwisted pairs too long at connector); cable run exceeds 90 m horizontal limit; low-quality cable or connectors used | Re-pull or re-route cable to correct bend issues; re-terminate with correct technique (maintain twist to within 13 mm of connector); verify cable run length; use cable and connectors from the same manufacturer and category |
| Labelling mismatch between patch panel and outlet | Labels not applied during installation; labels applied incorrectly; labels not updated after moves/adds/changes; different naming conventions used by different installers | Audit and correct labels using cable toner to trace each run; establish and enforce a single labelling standard; update labels immediately after every change; include labelling verification in the installation acceptance checklist |
| Poor cable management causing airflow blockage or damage | Cables not routed through trays or conduits; excessive cable bundles blocking rack ventilation; cables draped across ceiling tiles or floor without support; no cable management within racks | Install cable trays, conduits, or J-hooks along all cable routes; use vertical and horizontal cable managers within racks; enforce cable management standards in contractor handover criteria; conduct periodic cable audits |
| Fibre optic link failure or high attenuation | Dirty or scratched fibre connector end-face; excessive bend in fibre run; poor fusion splice; connector not fully seated; wrong fibre type connected (single-mode to multi-mode) | Clean connectors with lint-free wipes and IPA; inspect with fibre microscope; re-splice if loss exceeds threshold; verify fibre type matches at both ends; test with OTDR to locate fault point; protect fibre from physical stress |
| Outdated or missing cable records cause accidental disconnection | Cable records not updated after previous changes; no as-built diagram produced after installation; records kept in personal files rather than shared location | Mandate record updates as part of every change request closure; centralise records in cable management software or shared drive; conduct annual cable audit to reconcile records with physical infrastructure; include record update verification in handover checklist |

## Employability Skills

This CoCu develops the following employability skills through the work activities:

### 1. Communication Skills
- **Mapping:** Document cabling layouts, patch schedules, and as-built diagrams; present cabling plans and standards to management; communicate installation schedules and change requests to facilities teams and contractors

### 2. Teamwork & Collaboration
- **Mapping:** Coordinate with building contractors and facilities management during installation; collaborate with network teams on port assignments and VLAN configuration; manage handover between cabling installers and IT operations

### 3. Problem-solving
- **Mapping:** Diagnose cable certification failures and trace mislabelled or undocumented cables; resolve conflicts between cabling design and building constraints; address airflow and cable management issues in racks

### 4. Initiative & Self-reliance
- **Mapping:** Proactively audit cable records and labelling accuracy; take responsibility for maintaining up-to-date as-built documentation; initiate cable infrastructure improvements before capacity limits are reached

### 5. Planning & Organizing
- **Mapping:** Plan cabling layouts compliant with TIA/EIA-568 or ISO 11801 standards; organize cable installation schedules to minimize service disruption; manage contractor timelines and acceptance testing

### 6. Self-management & Safety Awareness
- **Mapping:** Follow building codes and fire safety requirements for cable routing; manage risk of accidental disconnection during moves/adds/changes; ensure safe handling of fibre optic equipment and fusion splicers

### 7. Technology Use & Technical Proficiency
- **Mapping:** Use cable certifiers, OTDR, and toner/probe tools for testing and tracing; operate fibre fusion splicers; manage cable infrastructure using software such as NetBox or Patch Manager

### 8. Learning Skills & Continuous Improvement
- **Mapping:** Stay current on cabling standards and new cable categories; analyze certification test results to improve installation quality; learn from cabling audits to refine documentation and labelling practices



## Attitude, Safety and Environmental

### Workplace Safety
- **Working at Height:** Use approved ladders, scaffolding, or mobile elevated work platforms when installing cables in ceiling voids, risers, or elevated cable trays. Follow organisational working-at-height procedures, including pre-use equipment inspection, three-point contact on ladders, and the buddy system. Never stand on chairs, desks, or unstable surfaces.
- **Confined Spaces and Ceiling Plenums:** Exercise caution when working in confined spaces such as riser shafts, floor voids, and ceiling plenums. Check for adequate ventilation, be aware of asbestos-containing materials in older buildings, and follow organisational confined-space entry procedures including sign-in/sign-out and emergency contacts. Report any suspected asbestos immediately and do not disturb it.
- **Fibre Optic Safety:** Never look into the end of a live fibre optic cable or transceiver, as laser light can cause permanent eye damage. Wear safety glasses when cleaving or splicing fibre to protect against glass splinter injuries. Collect and dispose of all fibre offcuts in a designated sharps container rather than general waste.
- **Cable Weight and Physical Handling:** Use proper lifting techniques and mechanical aids (cable reels on stands, cable pulling machines) when handling heavy cable drums and large cable bundles. Secure cable reels to prevent rolling during transport. Keep walkways and work areas clear of cable offcuts, tools, and packaging to prevent trip hazards during installation.

### Environmental Considerations
- **Sustainable IT Practices:** Plan for efficient resource utilisation (power, cooling, storage) when configuring server environments. Consider virtualisation and consolidation to reduce physical hardware footprint and energy consumption.
- **Responsible Asset Disposal:** Ensure decommissioned servers and storage devices are securely wiped and responsibly recycled through certified e-waste facilities.

### Professional Attitudes
- **Ethical Decision-Making:** Make recommendations based on technical merit and organisational benefit, not vendor preferences or personal convenience. Maintain transparency with stakeholders about trade-offs and costs.
- **Continuous Learning:** Stay current with new server technologies, security standards, and industry best practices to make informed decisions about infrastructure improvements.
- **Team Leadership:** Lead technical teams with respect and clear communication; ensure team members understand the rationale behind decisions and have opportunity to contribute expertise.
- **Accountability:** Take responsibility for system stability and performance; proactively monitor configurations and respond quickly to issues that could impact business operations.
