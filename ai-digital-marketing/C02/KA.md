### Section A (20 marks — 2 marks each)

### Section B

**B1** (8 marks — 4 marks for LLM explanation, 2 marks each limitation):
LLM explanation (4 marks): LLMs are trained on massive text datasets and learn statistical patterns of how words and sentences follow one another. They generate text token-by-token, predicting the most probable next word given the preceding context. They do not "understand" meaning in the human sense — they produce text that is statistically similar to their training data. They have no access to real-time information unless connected to web-browsing tools.
Limitations (2 marks each, accept any two):
(1) Hallucination — LLMs fabricate facts, statistics, and references with the same confident tone as accurate content; marketers must verify all factual claims before publishing.
(2) Knowledge cutoff — LLMs cannot access information published after their training cutoff; current market data, recent regulatory changes, and new competitor activities will not be in the output.
(3) Brand voice inconsistency — LLMs default to generic, polished English that may not match the brand's established tone; human editing is required to infuse brand personality.
(4) Cultural and linguistic nuance — LLMs may produce technically correct BM or Mandarin that sounds unnatural to Malaysian native speakers, particularly for colloquial or culturally specific expressions.

**B2** (8 marks — 2 marks per stage):
- D = Draft: Use the AI tool to generate an initial draft based on a well-structured prompt; the goal is to produce raw material quickly, not a final output.
- E = Edit: The content creator reviews the draft, corrects factual errors, removes hallucinations, and improves clarity and flow; this stage ensures accuracy.
- E = Enhance: Infuse the brand's voice, tone, and style; add Malaysia-specific context, cultural references, and calls-to-action that the AI may have missed; add SEO optimisation.
- P = Publish: Conduct final proofreading (grammar, spelling, formatting), apply plagiarism and AI-detection checks if required by client policy, then publish through the appropriate CMS or platform.

**B3** (8 marks — 3 marks for keyword research concept, 5 marks for process):
Keyword research concept (3 marks): the process of identifying the search terms potential customers use when looking for products or information; targets keywords with sufficient search volume, manageable competition, and clear relevance to the content.
Process (5 marks — 1 each step): (1) Seed keyword brainstorm: prompt AI "List 20 search queries a Malaysian secondary school student or parent would type into Google when looking for online tutoring in Malaysia." (2) Expand with free tools: enter seed keywords into Google Keyword Planner, Ubersuggest, or Answer The Public to find search volume and related terms. (3) Evaluate competition: use Keywords Everywhere browser extension to check keyword difficulty. (4) Select three target keywords based on a mix of search volume and achievable ranking difficulty — e.g., "online tutor Malaysia" (high volume, high competition), "SPM add maths tutor online" (medium volume, lower competition), "best online tuition Malaysia 2026" (lower volume, lower competition, high intent). (5) Map keywords to article sections: primary keyword in H1 and first paragraph; secondary keywords in H2 subheadings; LSI (related) terms distributed naturally in body text.

**B4** (8 marks — 2 marks each platform):
- a) Instagram: short (50–150 words ideal), hook in first line before "more" cutoff, conversational and aspirational tone, 3–10 relevant hashtags, strong visual reference in copy, emoji used selectively.
- b) Facebook: longer posts (100–300 words) perform well for organic reach; storytelling tone; no excessive hashtags (2–3 max); works well with a link preview; question or CTA to encourage comments.
- c) LinkedIn: formal and insights-driven; 150–300 words; first line must be a strong professional hook before the "see more" break; no slang or excessive emoji; data and lessons learned perform well; 3 relevant professional hashtags.
- d) TikTok hook (3 seconds): must grab attention instantly — short punchy statement, provocative question, or unexpected fact; spoken aloud in video so script must match natural speech; examples: "If you're studying for SPM, stop doing this one thing…" or "Malaysian students who do THIS score A+ in Add Maths."

**B5** (8 marks — 4 marks for process, 2 marks each quality check):
Process (4 marks): paste the finalised English article into the AI tool (Claude, ChatGPT, or DeepSeek for BM/Mandarin); use a specific translation prompt: "Translate this article into natural, professional Bahasa Malaysia suitable for a Malaysian skincare blog. Maintain the brand's friendly and educational tone. Do not translate product names or brand name." Repeat for Mandarin: "Translate into Simplified Chinese suitable for a Malaysian Chinese reader. Use natural, conversational Mandarin. Retain English product names in brackets after the Mandarin term."
Quality check 1 (2 marks): Native language review — have a fluent BM or Mandarin speaker review the translation for naturalness, cultural appropriateness, and any awkward phrasings that are technically correct but unnatural in the Malaysian context (e.g., AI may use Mainland Chinese Mandarin expressions unfamiliar to Malaysian Chinese readers).
Quality check 2 (2 marks): Factual accuracy check — verify that all product claims, statistics, and regulatory statements (e.g., KKM registration references) in the original article have been accurately translated and not altered or omitted during the AI translation process.

### Section C

**C1** (12 marks):

a) Brief, prompt, and editing (6 marks — 2 marks per component):
Brief (2 marks): Target keyword: "study tips for SPM students Malaysia"; Target audience: Malaysian students aged 15–18 preparing for SPM; Tone: encouraging, peer-to-peer, approachable; Word count: 700 words; Structure: intro + 5 tips with subheadings + conclusion with CTA; Mandatory inclusions: SPM context, Malaysian study culture references, at least one mention of AI study tools.
CRAFT prompt (2 marks): "Context: I am a content writer for Books2U, a Malaysian online bookstore targeting students aged 15–25. Role: You are an experienced educational content writer with expertise in Malaysian secondary school education and the SPM examination system. Audience: Malaysian secondary school students aged 15–18 preparing for SPM, browsing on mobile. Format: Write a 700-word SEO blog article with an H1 title, 5 H2 subheadings (one per tip), a 100-word introduction, and a 70-word conclusion with a CTA to visit our online bookstore. Task: Write the article 'Top 5 Study Tips for SPM Students in Malaysia' targeting the keyword 'study tips for SPM students Malaysia' in the H1 and first paragraph."
Editing steps (2 marks): Verify all facts (SPM subject names, exam dates, any statistics cited); adjust tone to match brand voice; check H1 includes primary keyword; add internal links to relevant book category pages; check readability (aim for Flesch-Kincaid Grade 8–10 for student audience); run plagiarism check if required by client; proofread for grammar and spelling.

b) Five on-page SEO elements (6 marks — 1.2 marks each):
1. H1 tag: must contain the primary keyword, appear only once, and clearly state the article topic.
2. Meta description: 150–160 characters; must include the primary keyword; written to encourage clicks from search results (not auto-generated).
3. URL slug: short, keyword-rich, hyphenated: `/study-tips-spm-students-malaysia` — not `/blog/article?id=1234`.
4. Internal links: at least 2 links to related content on the same website (e.g., link to "Best Reference Books for SPM" product category); improves site structure and time-on-site.
5. Image alt text: any images used must have descriptive alt text containing the keyword naturally (e.g., `alt="SPM student studying with reference books Malaysia"`); improves image search visibility and accessibility.

**C3** (14 marks):

a) Editorial review process (7 marks):
Step 1 — Factual audit (2 marks): List every factual claim in the article (trail names, distances, difficulty ratings, operating hours, access status). For each claim, verify against a minimum of two authoritative sources: Sabah Tourism Board official website, Parks Malaysia / Sabah Parks official portal, recent travel blogs or news articles dated within the past 12 months. Mark unverified or contradicted claims for correction or removal. Step 2 — Current status check (2 marks): Specifically for any trail listed as accessible, cross-check with Parks Malaysia (sabahparks.org.my) for current closure notices. The article's incorrect open/closed status is a safety risk to readers — this is a non-negotiable correction. Step 3 — Authenticity and voice improvement (2 marks): Replace generic AI phrases ("breathtaking views", "an unforgettable experience") with specific Malaysian references: local names for landmarks, correct BM terms for trail features, realistic advice about transport from Kota Kinabalu city, and references to local guiding operators. Prompt AI: "Rewrite this paragraph in a voice that sounds like a local Malaysian hiker sharing personal experience, not a travel brochure." Step 4 — Final proofreading (1 mark): Check grammar, spelling (including correct BM terms where used), and factual consistency across the full article before submission for publication.

b) Mandarin version and multilingual challenges (7 marks — 4 marks for process, 3 marks challenges):
Process (4 marks): Once the English article is fully fact-checked and edited, prompt DeepSeek or Claude: "Translate this article into Simplified Chinese suitable for a Malaysian Chinese reader in Sabah. Use natural, readable Mandarin. Retain English trail names in brackets after the Chinese term where a standard Chinese name does not exist. Do not translate proper nouns such as 'Kinabalu Park' or 'Sabah Parks.'" Have a Mandarin-fluent reviewer check the output before publication. Re-verify that all translated factual claims (distances, altitudes, permit requirements) have not been altered during translation.
Three multilingual challenges (3 marks — 1 each):
(1) Malaysian Chinese vs Mainland Chinese expressions: AI models trained predominantly on Simplified Chinese from China may use vocabulary and expressions unfamiliar or unnatural to Malaysian Chinese readers (e.g., different terms for hiking gear, trail difficulty, or cultural references). Human review by a Malaysian Chinese reader is essential.
(2) Untranslatable proper nouns and mixed-language names: Malaysian trail names, permit systems, and local operator names often have no standard Mandarin equivalent; inconsistent handling (sometimes translated, sometimes transliterated, sometimes left in English) creates reader confusion and must be standardised before publication.
(3) Factual accuracy through translation: AI may paraphrase rather than translate factual statements, subtly altering distances, permit fees, or regulatory requirements during translation. Every numeric and regulatory claim must be spot-checked against the English source after translation — not assumed to be identical.

**Q21.** [SENARIO / SCENARIO] A digital marketing trainee uses ChatGPT to write a product description for a Malaysian supplement brand. The AI states the supplement "has been clinically proven to reduce cholesterol by 30%." The trainee publishes it without checking. Explain the legal and professional risks involved and what the trainee should have done. [CoCU C02.1.1 / K2.1 / K2.3]

*Jawapan Contoh / Sample Answer:* Publishing an unverified health claim from an AI output creates multiple serious risks. First, the claim may be an AI hallucination — a fabricated statistic with no supporting clinical study. Second, under Malaysia's Control of Drugs and Cosmetics Regulations 1984 and MCMC content guidelines, making false health claims about supplements is a regulatory offence that can result in product seizure and criminal penalties. Third, the brand faces consumer trust and reputational damage if the claim is exposed as false. The trainee should have: verified the claim against the supplement manufacturer's registered KKM product dossier or published peer-reviewed clinical studies; removed the specific percentage claim if no verified source existed; replaced it with the approved registered health claim (if any) as stated on the product's KKM registration. The brand's legal team or a compliance officer should review health and efficacy claims before publication.

**Q29.** [SENARIO / SCENARIO] Explain what Generative Engine Optimisation (GEO) is, why it is increasingly important for Malaysian content creators, and describe FIVE (5) content strategies that increase the likelihood of AI answer engines citing or quoting a piece of content. [CoCU C02.2.2 / K2.5]

*Jawapan Contoh / Sample Answer:* GEO (Generative Engine Optimisation) is the emerging practice of structuring and writing content so that AI-powered answer engines — such as Perplexity AI, ChatGPT's browsing feature, Google Gemini, and Google AI Overviews — are more likely to cite, quote, or synthesise information from that content when answering user queries. It differs from traditional SEO in that the goal is not only to rank on a search results page, but to be the source that an AI assistant uses when generating its answer. For Malaysian content creators, GEO is increasingly important because: (1) Malaysian internet users are adopting AI assistants rapidly; (2) AI Overviews are appearing at the top of Google search results in Malaysia for an increasing proportion of queries; (3) being cited by an AI assistant provides implicit authority and trust signals that drive brand recognition even when users do not click through. Five GEO content strategies: (1) Direct answer format — open articles with a clear, concise paragraph that directly answers the likely search question, ideally within the first 100 words; AI answer engines extract these as quick-answer summaries. (2) Structured data and FAQ sections — include an explicit FAQ section with question-and-answer format; AI models prefer content that already presents information in a Q&A structure. (3) Authoritative and citable writing style — use precise language, named sources, specific statistics (with citations), and avoid vague claims; AI engines prefer citable, specific content over marketing fluff. (4) Entity coverage — comprehensively cover the main entities (people, organisations, products, locations) related to the topic; content that covers a topic more completely than competitors is more likely to be selected as the source. (5) E-E-A-T signals — author bio with credentials, organisation page with contact details, citations of primary sources; AI engines favour content from demonstrated subject-matter experts and trusted organisations.

**Q30.** [SENARIO / SCENARIO] A digital marketing trainee claims: "Publishing AI-generated content immediately saves time and that's what matters." *Jawapan Contoh / Sample Answer:* The claim reflects a misunderstanding of what AI content tools produce and the professional standards required in Malaysian digital marketing practice. In Malaysia, publishing a false health claim about a product can violate the Control of Drugs and Cosmetics Regulations 1984 and MCMC content standards, resulting in fines, product delisting, or criminal proceedings. Time saved publishing immediately is insignificant compared to the cost of a regulatory enforcement action. (2) Copyright risk — AI models may reproduce phrases or sentences from their training data that are substantially similar to copyrighted works. The publisher, not the AI, bears legal liability under Malaysia's Copyright Act 1987. A plagiarism check takes minutes; a copyright lawsuit takes years. (3) Brand voice damage — unedited AI defaults to generic, often Western-centric writing that may not resonate with Malaysian audiences. A brand's voice and tone are commercially valuable assets; generic content dilutes brand differentiation and erodes audience trust over time. (4) SEO penalties — AI-generated content that lacks depth, original insight, or human editing signals low quality to Google's Helpful Content System, which actively demotes content it determines was produced primarily for search engines rather than for genuine human value. Fast publication of thin AI content can actively harm search rankings. (5) Professional duty of care — a digital marketing professional who publishes unverified AI content on behalf of a client is in breach of the professional duty to ensure accuracy, legality, and brand integrity in all deliverables. The client hired a professional, not an AI prompt runner.
